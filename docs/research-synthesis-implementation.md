# Research Synthesis & Implementation Decisions — After 10 Iterations

309 sources logged, 10 research passes, roughly a dozen distinct source ecosystems (academic optimization literature, OT-deception academia, commercial vendors, US national labs, a decade of NPS theses, a federal analysis tool, EU-funded projects, an industry R&D consortium). This document does three things: states my actual confidence level and where it's shaky, synthesizes the whole picture into one mental model, and converts that into specific decisions for the project rather than a menu of options.

## Confidence Assessment

**High confidence — verified via full-text reads or consistent multi-source confirmation:**
- Existing OT deception tools and platforms (open-source, academic, commercial, national-lab) are overwhelmingly about deception *generation and fidelity* — making a convincing decoy — and decide *where* to deploy it manually, by template, or by architectural convention. This held up identically across every source type checked.
- A mature, decades-deep body of formal placement-*optimization* math exists (MILP, greedy with approximation guarantees, Stackelberg/game-theoretic, POSG, GNN, evolutionary) — but it was built almost entirely for enterprise IT, Active Directory, cloud/microservices, and IoT, not OT/ICS.
- IEC 62443 zones and conduits are used procedurally and architecturally everywhere this was checked (risk-assessment papers, vendor guides, the standard itself) — never as a formal, numeric input to an optimization algorithm, in deception or general OT security placement.
- Several specific, independently-sourced components now exist that can genuinely be built on: a submodularity-based approximation guarantee for a greedy algorithm, two independent OT-specific criticality-scoring approaches, a real ablation-tested justification for weighting operational risk heavily, and a real architectural precedent for a deterministic-optimizer-plus-LLM-explainer design.

**Moderate confidence — this is the one that matters most, so read it carefully:**
- The novelty claim overall survived nine iterations of "no OT-specific placement optimization found" — and then iteration 10 found the closest thing yet (Devika Jay, IEEE Access 2023), via a sector-specific pivot that hadn't been tried before. That should recalibrate you, not just add one more citation to a list. Nine iterations of absence is real evidence, but it isn't proof, and the tenth iteration is a direct demonstration of that: the gap narrowed the moment a genuinely new search angle was tried. I do not have systematic database coverage (a certified IEEE Xplore/Scopus/Web of Science sweep) — I have broad, persistent, multi-angle web search across ten passes, which is thorough but not the same instrument. My honest estimate: it is likely, not certain, that nothing closer than the Jay paper exists in the specific combination you're proposing (multi-zone, IEC-62443-structured, composite-objective). A water-sector or manufacturing-sector equivalent of the Jay paper — found the same way electric-utility's was, by pivoting sectors — is a real possibility I can't rule out.

**Lower confidence / genuinely open:**
- Whether the three distinctions I've drawn between your project and the Jay paper (scope, standard, objective structure) hold up against her actual mathematical formulation — I've only verified this against the abstract, not the full text.
- Feasibility specifics for your actual weekend-hours implementation. I have comparable-scale data from nine iterations (testbed sizes, solver runtimes, evaluation scope), which is useful calibration — but the final call on what's buildable in your actual hours is yours and your guide's, not something a literature search can settle.

## The Refined Novelty Claim

State it this precisely in section 14, not more broadly:

> To the best of a broad, multi-angle literature search (not a certified systematic database review), no published work performs formal, quantitative deception-placement optimization across a multi-zone OT/ICS network architecture using IEC 62443 zone/conduit structure and asset criticality as first-class inputs to a composite objective (attack-path coverage, early detection, criticality-weighted protection, operational risk, and deployment cost), validated against baseline placement strategies. The closest identified prior work (Jay, 2023) performs formally-optimized, game-theoretic decoy allocation within a single digital substation's internal VLAN, tied to IEC 61850/GOOSE protocol semantics — a narrower, single-zone, single-protocol problem than the multi-zone, protocol-agnostic, IEC-62443-structured problem addressed here.

That's a claim you can defend line by line, because every clause in it is something ten iterations actually tested.

## Implementation Decisions

This is where the research gets used rather than just cited. Recommendations, not options, with the evidence behind each.

**Optimization method — start with greedy, validate with MILP at your scale, hold genetic algorithms in reserve.** Placement is proven NP-hard even on static graphs (Ngo et al.), which rules out brute-force. A separate paper (Kulkarni et al.) proves a comparable decoy-placement objective is submodular in structured cases, which gives greedy a real (1−1/e) approximation guarantee rather than "greedy because it's simple" — check whether your own objective satisfies the same property before claiming the guarantee, but the proof *pattern* is directly reusable. At the ~20-60-node scale that's realistic for weekend-hours testbed work, an exact MILP formulation is also tractable and gives you an optimality gap to validate the greedy result against. Reserve a genetic-algorithm comparison as a secondary method if time allows — it has precedent in this literature but isn't where your core contribution should live.

**Criticality scoring — combine both routes found, don't pick one.** Use IEC 62443 Security Level vectors (the 7-element FR1–FR7 structure) as a standards-grounded base score — and say explicitly, citing the field's own acknowledgment (exida), that SL quantification is a semi-quantitative approximation, not a solved measurement. Multiply or adjust that base score by a network-centrality-and-operational-damage factor, following the worked pattern in the criticality-analysis patent (locality × centrality × damage). One is defensible because it's standards-based; the other because it's computable directly from the network model you're already building. Citing both, and noting they capture different things (required protection level vs. actual network/operational importance), is stronger than picking one.

**Candidate deception locations — filter before you optimize, don't optimize over every node.** Adapt the four-criterion plausibility rubric from the "Decoys Cannot Go Everywhere" critique (can a decoy be placed here; would an attacker plausibly reach it; does interaction yield useful signal; is the interaction a reliable indicator of malice) to the MITRE ATT&CK *ICS* matrix specifically, since that audit has only been done for the enterprise matrix so far. Add one more filter your literature review surfaced that the original rubric doesn't cover: basic detectability. A decoy at a heavily-scanned position that's trivially fingerprinted (via TTL, port-count, or other passive signatures — this is a real, demonstrated attacker capability) may be worse than no decoy there. State this as an assumption in your threat model rather than solving honeypot-fingerprinting yourself.

**Objective function — the original five terms hold up, weight operational risk more than intuition suggests.** Coverage, early detection, and cost are standard across nearly every paper found and need no further justification. Criticality uses the combined method above. For operational risk: a dynamic-deception RL paper that ablates its own reward function found removing the false-positive-control term caused a 51.4% performance drop — the single most important term in their model. That's real evidence this term deserves serious weight, not a minor correction. For deriving the actual weights: the field's standard practice is a parametric sensitivity sweep (vary each coefficient, report how the outcome shifts), not formal expert elicitation, which has no precedent anywhere in this literature. Your original plan already matches this — run the sweep, report it, and that's your justification.

**Baselines — random and betweenness-centrality, stated with awareness of their limits.** Both are established comparators in this exact literature. Be explicit that you're using *betweenness* specifically, because that's the most common choice in comparable work — and cite, without needing to implement, recent work arguing standard centrality measures miss source/intermediate/target role structure in lateral-movement scenarios. Citing the critique while using the standard baseline is a stronger position than either ignoring the critique or trying to implement a fix to it within your scope.

**AI layer — deterministic optimizer decides, LLM only explains, and you now have a citable precedent for exactly that split.** A 2026 paper (i-EXAM/SPEAR) does almost precisely your section-9 architecture for general network hardening: a formal planner identifies strategies with soundness guarantees, an LLM's only job is explaining them in natural language. A separate paper bridges that same planning formalism directly to IT-to-OT attack-path discovery. Together they mean your "AI explains, doesn't decide" design isn't a constraint you're defending from scratch — it's a recognized pattern with recent precedent, one step removed from your exact domain. Implement it as originally scoped: local model via Ollama, prompted with the optimizer's own output (which locations, which metrics drove the choice, comparison to baselines) to generate the analyst-facing explanation.

**Testbed scope — keep the physical build modest, let the modeled topology be somewhat richer than what's physically deployed.** Comparable published work validates at tens to low-thousands of nodes; your original proposed architecture (OT DMZ, engineering workstation, HMI, control LAN, two PLCs) is on the small end of that range, which is appropriate for weekend-hours availability. Consider modeling a slightly richer network *graph* (15–25 assets/zones) than you physically deploy — not every modeled node needs a running VM; some can be graph-only nodes that make the placement problem topologically interesting without multiplying your build effort.

**Section 14 — who gets named, and how much space each gets.** Devika Jay's paper gets its own paragraph; it's the closest thing found and needs the scope/standard/objective distinctions argued explicitly, not listed alongside everything else. Zambianco et al. gets substantial treatment as the closest *methodological* precedent (graph-based optimization using MITRE ATT&CK), explicitly distinguished by domain. DecIED and Decepti-SCADA get shorter treatment as OT deception systems that don't formally optimize placement. CICAT and securiCAD get cited as evidence the *analysis* layer (attack-path generation, criticality scoring) is mature and validated, stopping short of where your contribution begins. The four gap-confirmations (Sandia, EPRI, the general and smart-grid-specific surveys) support the novelty claim directly and are worth a combined sentence rather than four separate ones.
